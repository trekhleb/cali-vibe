Write a vivid, insight-packed snapshot of {PLACE_NAME} ({PLACE_TYPE}) in California for someone considering moving there. Use the metrics below AND widely known, verifiable facts about the area's geography, major employers, and physical infrastructure.

Goal: Produce the kind of paragraph someone reads at lunch and immediately retells to a friend — specific, surprising, and grounded. Every sentence should make the reader think "huh, I didn't know that."

Voice: A sharp, well-traveled analyst who has studied both the numbers and the landscape. Confident, warm, occasionally witty — never snarky or dismissive. You may reference real, verifiable things about the area (terrain, local economy, food scene, parks, commute patterns) alongside the data, but never invent or guess — if you're uncertain, omit it.

What to weave in (pick the 3–4 most interesting for THIS place — don't try to cover all):
- Terrain and setting: hilly or flat, canyon-carved, valley floor, coastal bluffs? Does the geography give residents sunset views over western ridgelines, bay panoramas, or open-sky farm horizons?
- Pacing and activity: does the place feel quiet-suburban, bustling-urban, sleepy-rural, or college-town? Is the evening culture centered around lively downtown sidewalks, or quiet residential streets? Describe pedestrian convenience, trail access, and connectedness of streets and parks — but do not make generalized claims about whether residents feel safe at night.
- Neighborhood cohesion: does the area feel whole and uniformly walkable, or is it fragmented — pockets separated by highway overpasses, industrial stretches, or abrupt transitions? Are there well-connected parks and trails, or do you drive between isolated subdivisions?
- Employment and commute: is this a bedroom community or a job center? What industries dominate — tech, healthcare, agriculture, entertainment, logistics, defense, education? Can you switch employers without switching zip codes? What does the commute look like?
- Affordability reality: at the median income, who realistically affords to buy vs. rent? Is the local economy driven by tech, public-sector roles, healthcare, or the service industry? Frame in terms of local industries and housing costs, never demographics or age groups.
- Local flavor: what's the food and retail scene signal? Proximity to quality grocery (farmer's markets, specialty stores) and dining variety — sit-down restaurants, local coffee spots, fast-casual options? Does the area feel like it has its own identity or could it be anywhere?
- Nature and outdoors: parks, trails, open space, proximity to ocean or mountains. Can you go for a casual evening walk somewhere pleasant?
- Weather and sunshine: use the climate data to paint what daily life actually feels like across seasons.

Structure: Open with the single most surprising or delightful detail — the thing that makes someone say "wait, really?" Vary your opening structure — never start two descriptions the same way (no repeated "Here's what catches people off guard" or similar formula). Then weave 2–3 tradeoffs naturally using both data and verifiable local knowledge. Close with an honest one-sentence observation that captures the essential character of the place — the reader should think "that's interesting," not "they're trying to sell me this place."

What to avoid:
- Generic observations that could describe any suburb
- Listing facts without narrative tissue
- Promotional or booster-ish language
- Anything you are not reasonably confident is accurate — omit rather than guess
- Ending with a sales pitch or booster closing line — the last sentence should be a neutral observation, not an endorsement. Never imply the place is "worth it," "unlike anywhere else," "impossible to replicate," or otherwise special in a way that encourages moving there. A good closer names a defining tension or tradeoff; a bad closer sells.
- Phrases that imply unverifiable first-hand knowledge: "locals say," "people here tend to," "this area is known for," "it feels like," "residents will tell you." State facts or omit.
- Do not make inferences about residents' behavior, feelings, or motivations — e.g., "most buyers arrive with existing equity," "residents think about parking differently," "a six-figure salary can feel middle-class." Stick to what the data shows (price-to-income ratios, rent levels, crime rates) and let the reader draw their own conclusions.
- Do not contradict the numerical data provided at the end of this prompt. If your general knowledge conflicts with the provided metrics, defer entirely to the supplied numbers.

Content safety — strict rules:
- Never reference the racial, ethnic, or religious composition of a place — even indirectly or positively ("diverse community", "large X population", "cultural melting pot")
- Never discourage or encourage moving based on who lives there
- Never imply a place is unsafe for specific groups of people
- Never reference specific cultural communities, enclaves, or ethnic neighborhoods
- Never use language that could be interpreted as housing discrimination or steering under the Fair Housing Act
- If crime data is mentioned, frame it around infrastructure, policy, or statistics — never stigmatize residents or neighborhoods by name
- No political commentary or partisan framing
- Do not name specific neighborhoods as "sketchy", "rough", "bad", or similar
- When discussing affordability, frame around income levels and housing cost ratios — never around who "belongs" or "doesn't belong." Do not name specific job titles or occupations as examples of who can or cannot afford to live there (e.g., "even senior engineers struggle to buy," "software engineers often rent"). Use income-to-price ratios and let readers map to their own situation.
- When discussing local character or vibe, describe the economic and professional texture — never the cultural or ethnic identity
- Never use terms that imply a preference for certain types of residents: "family-friendly", "great for young professionals", "ideal for retirees", "empty nesters", "bachelor", "exclusive", "traditional", "starter-home area", "safe for [any group]", "quiet mature area", "where you'd fit in"
- If referencing education data, cite test scores or graduation rates as statistics only — never use school quality as a proxy for neighborhood quality (e.g., do not write "good schools" or "poor school district" as shorthand for what an area is like)
- Do NOT invent or guess any names of local businesses, parks, or landmarks. If you include a specific name, it must be a widely recognized, major geographical or cultural anchor of the city (e.g., "Mount Diablo", "Balboa Park"). Otherwise, describe amenities generically (e.g., "a walkable main street", "local hiking trails").
- If this place shares a name with its county (e.g., San Francisco, Los Angeles, Orange), focus on what makes the *city* experience distinct from the broader county — different density, different neighborhoods, different daily texture.

Format: One paragraph, roughly 150–200 words. Return only the paragraph text — no title, no heading, no quotes, no preamble.
